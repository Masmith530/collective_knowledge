This article covers the flight controls in full — what each one does, and the
mechanism that makes it happen. [Ground 2](/curriculum/private-helicopter/l02p1-flight-controls/)
introduces enough of this to put your hands on the controls; this is the rest of it.

<Aside type="caution">
Mechanisms vary between airframes. This describes the common arrangement in a
light piston helicopter with a teetering main rotor, which is what most students
learn in. Your aircraft's POH Section 7 is authoritative for your aircraft.
</Aside>

## The problem the controls have to solve

A rotor blade produces lift as a function of its angle of attack. To control a
helicopter you need to change that angle — sometimes on every blade at once,
sometimes on each blade differently depending on where it is in its rotation.

That is the whole job:

- **Change every blade's pitch equally** → the rotor produces more or less total
  thrust → the helicopter climbs or descends. This is **collective** pitch.
- **Change each blade's pitch depending on its azimuth** (where it is around the
  circle) → the rotor produces more thrust on one side than the other → the disc
  tilts → the helicopter moves. This is **cyclic** pitch.

Everything mechanical in the control system exists to deliver those two
behaviours through the same set of blades at the same time.

## The swashplate

The swashplate is the component that translates your stationary control inputs
into pitch changes on blades that are spinning several hundred times a minute.

It is two plates riding on a common bearing around the mast:

- The **stationary star** (lower) does not rotate. Your cyclic and collective
  push-pull tubes connect here.
- The **rotating star** (upper) turns with the mast. **Pitch links** run from it
  up to the pitch horns on each blade grip.

Because the two plates share a bearing, the rotating star copies the stationary
star's *attitude and height* while still turning with the rotor.

That gives two independent movements:

| Swashplate movement | What the blades do | Control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slides **up or down** the mast | Every pitch link rises or falls equally, so all blades change pitch by the same amount | Collective |
| **Tilts** about the mast | Each pitch link rises and falls once per revolution, so each blade's pitch cycles with azimuth | Cyclic |

### Collective pitch

Raise the collective and the whole swashplate slides up the mast. Every pitch
link is pushed up by the same distance, so every blade increases pitch by the
same amount, everywhere in its rotation. Total rotor thrust increases.

This also increases rotor drag, which is why collective and power are
inseparable — see [Throttle, correlator, and governor](#throttle-correlator-and-governor).

### Cyclic pitch

Move the cyclic and the swashplate tilts. Now the rotating star is high on one
side of the circle and low on the other. A given blade therefore gets more pitch
as it passes through one part of its rotation and less as it passes through the
opposite part — once per revolution, continuously.

The side of the disc with more pitch produces more lift. The disc tilts toward
the side producing less, thrust tilts with it, and the helicopter accelerates in
that direction.

<Aside type="tip" title="The disc is the useful abstraction">
You are not steering the fuselage. You are tilting the rotor disc, and the
fuselage follows because it is hanging underneath the thrust vector. Thinking in
terms of "where is the disc pointed" makes most control questions answer
themselves.
</Aside>

## Phase lag, and why rigging hides it

Here is the part that confuses nearly everyone, and it is worth getting straight
once.

A rotor blade is a rotating mass. Apply a force to it and the maximum
*displacement* does not occur where you applied the force — it occurs roughly 90°
later in the direction of rotation. In a fully articulated rotor this is usually
explained through **gyroscopic precession**; in a flapping rotor it is more
accurately the result of the blade responding to a once-per-revolution pitch
change against its own flapping dynamics. Either way the practical consequence is
the same:

> To tilt the disc in a given direction, the maximum pitch change must be applied
> about 90° of rotation *before* that direction.

So why does pushing the cyclic forward tilt the disc forward, rather than 90° off?

**Because the designers already accounted for it.** The pitch links, pitch horns,
and swashplate are deliberately rigged so that the mechanical phase offset
cancels the aerodynamic one. The disc goes where the stick goes.

This matters for two reasons:

1. **You never have to think about it in flight**, and you should not try to.
2. **You will be asked about it on a checkride**, because it explains why the
   control linkage looks the way it does rather than being a simple direct
   connection.

## Pedals and the tail rotor

The pedals do the same thing the collective does, but to the tail rotor: they
change the pitch of the tail rotor blades collectively, all at once.

The linkage runs back to a pitch change mechanism on the tail rotor gearbox,
which slides along the tail rotor shaft and changes the blades' pitch through
their own pitch links.

More tail rotor pitch means more anti-torque thrust, which yaws the nose against
main rotor torque. Less pitch means less thrust, and the nose yaws with torque.

Note what this means: **the tail rotor is always producing thrust in normal
flight**, and that thrust costs engine power. A pedal input is a power demand
change, which is one reason pedal and collective inputs interact.

## Throttle, correlator, and governor

In a piston helicopter the throttle is on the end of the collective, and it is a
flight control, not a setting.

The rotor must stay within a narrow RPM band. But raising the collective
increases rotor drag, which would slow the rotor down unless power increases at
the same time. Three things manage this:

- **Your hand.** Rolling the throttle on as the collective comes up. This is the
  underlying skill, and it is why governor-off practice exists.
- **The correlator.** A mechanical linkage between collective and throttle that
  adds approximate throttle as the collective rises. It is a rough
  feed-forward — it gets you close, not correct.
- **The governor.** An electro-mechanical system that senses engine RPM and
  trims the throttle to hold it. It corrects what the correlator approximates.

<Aside type="caution" title="What the governor does not do">
The governor manages **engine RPM within its operating range**. It is not a
substitute for your own power management, it can be overwhelmed by an aggressive
collective input, it does not protect you from overspeeding or drooping the rotor
in all conditions, and it may be required to be off for certain training. Fly as
though you are responsible for RPM, because you are.
</Aside>

## Secondary control interactions

Every control affects more than its primary axis. This is a deeper pass over what
L2 introduces.

**Collective → yaw.** Changing collective changes main rotor torque, so the
fuselage yaws. In a hover this is immediate and needs pedal to correct. In
forward flight it is smaller, because the vertical stabilizer is doing some of
the anti-torque work aerodynamically.

**Collective → pitch attitude.** Raising collective increases thrust, and because
the thrust vector generally does not pass exactly through the CG, a pitch change
follows. Which direction depends on the aircraft and the flight condition.

**Pedal → power required.** More tail rotor pitch means more power to the tail
rotor, so there is less available to the main rotor. At high power settings a
large pedal input can cost you rotor RPM.

**Cyclic → power required.** Tilting the disc changes the induced flow through
it, so holding altitude through a cyclic change generally needs a power change
too.

**Airspeed → pedal.** As airspeed increases, the vertical stabilizer provides
more directional stability and less pedal is needed to hold heading.

The practical summary: **there is no single-axis input in a helicopter.** Every
input is a small package of corrections, which is why coordination rather than
individual control movement is the skill being trained.

## Friction, trim, and feel

- **Collective friction** holds the collective where you put it against its own
  tendency to creep. Enough to stop drift, never enough that you have to fight it.
- **Cyclic friction** does the same for the cyclic. On most light helicopters it
  is set very light or off in flight.
- **Trim systems**, where fitted, reduce the steady force you have to hold. On
  light piston helicopters this may be as simple as a bungee or spring
  arrangement.

Most light training helicopters have **no hydraulic boost on the main rotor
controls** — you feel the rotor directly through the cyclic, including feedback
from the blades. On larger helicopters hydraulic servos isolate you from that,
which changes the feel substantially and introduces hydraulic failure as a
systems concern.

## Why smooth inputs are a structural requirement

Smooth control inputs are not a politeness or a comfort preference. On a
**teetering** rotor head, the rotor is free to teeter about a central hinge, and
there are **teeter stops** limiting that travel.

With the rotor loaded normally, cyclic tilts the disc. With the rotor
**unloaded** — low-G — there is little thrust for the cyclic to work against, so
a cyclic input moves the *fuselage* relative to the rotor instead. Enough of
that, particularly a lateral input, and the hub contacts the mast:
**mast bumping**, which can be catastrophic and can separate the rotor from the
aircraft.

The chain is: abrupt input → unloaded rotor → cyclic no longer controls the disc →
hub strikes mast. The first link is the one you control.

This is why collective is *always* raised and lowered slowly, why cyclic inputs
in stable flight are wrist movements rather than arm movements, and why the
recovery from low-G is **gentle aft cyclic** and never lateral cyclic.
